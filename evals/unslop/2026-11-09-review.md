# Conversation coverage review

Reviewed on 9 November 2026 using the prompts in [the evaluation guide](../unslop.md).

Method: instruction review followed by a manual, single-agent exercise using the revised skill. The samples below were written and assessed in the same session that edited the skill, with existing session and repo instructions active. They are useful checks and examples, not an independent evaluation, controlled before/after comparison or evidence of consistency across models and harnesses.

## Gaps addressed

- The original skill focused on editing prose. It now explicitly covers conversation, explanations, advice and creative work.
- Mandatory complete sentences and outcome-first replies could make casual or emotional interactions stiff. The revised defaults allow brief fragments and acknowledgement before advice.
- Cutting generic language could remove a perfectly suitable conversational response. The rule now targets empty explanation and stock padding.
- Preserving every quotation conflicted with rewriting a passage supplied in quotes. The revised rule distinguishes literal strings from the text being edited.
- Broad bans on metaphors and decorative formatting could override an analogy or a requested creative voice. User-requested style and formats now explicitly take precedence.
- Follow-up context, respectful disagreement, focused questions and truthful warmth now have direct guidance.

The skill remains approximately the same length: 784 words before, 792 after. Evaluation material stays outside the skill.

## Sample outputs and assessment

### Filler and spelling

You can now customise your dashboard by organising your favourite colours. Saved preferences apply only to new projects; existing projects are unaffected.

Assessment: retains the feature and restriction, uses UK spelling and removes promotional padding.

### Protected strings

To customise the colour, set `color: "gray"` in `src/color-picker.ts`. Run `npm run analyze`. The API returns {"favorite": true}. The button currently says "Customize colors". This behaviour is documented by the World Health Organization.

Assessment: changes original prose while preserving code, JSON, the quoted label and proper name. This is an edit of supplied documentation, not independent verification of its claim.

### Evidence and uncertainty

In a single staging run, median latency fell from 240 ms to 180 ms with the cache. The error rate was unchanged. We haven't tested production latency yet, so we don't know whether the improvement will carry over.

Assessment: retains the measurements and limits; removes the unsupported appeal to experts without inventing a source.

### Voice and technical meaning

Tiny victory: the vector search no longer times out on my test dataset. I've labelled 12 examples, but that's nowhere near enough to call this reliable. Fancy trying it with your dataset tomorrow?

Assessment: makes a small spelling and flow edit without removing the writer's personality, invitation or caution.

### New response

The settings page now saves colour preferences for new projects. Existing projects keep their previous settings. We've checked two new projects and one existing project; we don't have production results yet.

Assessment: includes the behaviour, scope and verification limits. The first-person verification statement is appropriate within the requested drafted update, based on supplied facts; it would be misleading as a claim that the assistant ran those checks.

### Brief acknowledgement

You're welcome.

Assessment: no recap, headings or unnecessary follow-up offer. A familiar phrase can still be the natural response.

### Frustration

That's a rough way to lose a weekend. We can leave the troubleshooting for another day.

Assessment: recognises the situation and respects the request for no debugging steps. Avoids claiming shared experience or providing forced reassurance.

### Honest disagreement

The median latency was 25% lower in that staging run, yes. That doesn't establish a 25% improvement in production; you'd need production measurements to make that claim.

Assessment: distinguishes the correct calculation from the unsupported conclusion without simply agreeing or dismissing the result.

### Beginner explanation

A database index is like the index at the back of a book. It helps the database find matching records without checking every record in a table.

For example, an index on an email column can help find an account by its email address. The trade-off is that adding or changing records takes extra work because the database must update the index too.

Assessment: preserves a useful analogy, explains the mechanism and gives one practical trade-off in plain language.

### Follow-up context

I'd stick with SQLite. A search box for names or titles fits your local, single-user prototype. If you later need full-text search across longer documents, SQLite's full-text search support is worth considering before changing databases.

Assessment: builds on the existing choice and distinguishes search needs without reopening the entire decision or asking redundant questions. Actual full-text search availability depends on the SQLite build.

### Requested voice and format

You can pick your own colours now. Go wild 🎨

Assessment: nine words, one emoji, UK spelling and no wrapper. Edits the quoted input and follows the requested voice instead of applying the default against decorative emoji.

### Missing information

What matters most to you in the move, and how does the offer compare with your current situation?

Assessment: asks about the factors needed for a recommendation rather than making one up. Keeps the next turn manageable instead of presenting a questionnaire.

## Remaining uncertainty

These checks support the changes but do not prove that every interaction will improve. In real use, watch for warmth becoming repetitive, short answers becoming curt, questions being skipped when they matter, and uncertain claims becoming too confident. Use fresh-session comparisons to assess those behaviours across repeated runs. The skill affects responses only when the surrounding system supplies its instructions.
